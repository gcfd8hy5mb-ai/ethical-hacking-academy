01 / Scope & authorization

Design the red-team engagement before touching a system

A legitimate assessment starts with written authorization, not a scanner. Your task is to translate a fictional client's objectives into a bounded plan that a defender can safely supervise.

Scenario

Northbridge Learning, a fictional company, owns LAB-WIN01, LAB-LNX01 and a training web portal at training.example.test on an isolated network. The engagement goal is to evaluate whether lab monitoring identifies preapproved benign discovery commands and whether incident escalation follows policy. Production systems, employee accounts, external domains, phishing, persistence, exploitation and denial of service are all out of scope.

Practical workflow

  1. Write the purpose, named system owners, allowed dates, permitted source addresses and stop conditions. State the exact test systems, not a broad network range.
  2. Specify that only read-only commands and synthetic logs are approved. Decide what to do if an unexpected real account or production hostname appears.
  3. Assign an observer and a point of contact who can pause the activity. Identify which event logs may be examined and how to redact personal information.
  4. Create three measurable success criteria: host identification accuracy, evidence capture quality and response escalation timing.

Synthetic evidence / worked example

Authorization: fictional lab systems only
Included: LAB-WIN01, LAB-LNX01, training.example.test
Allowed: passive inventory, approved local read-only commands, synthetic-log analysis
Excluded: production, credential capture, exfiltration, exploitation, persistence, disruption
Stop: real customer data encountered; scope ambiguity; system instability
Reporting: UTC timeline + redacted evidence + defensive recommendations

Interpretation

Scope is an enforceable boundary. If an observed system is outside the written list, stop rather than assuming adjacency implies permission.

Troubleshooting and limitations: When a host name differs from the scope document, verify the owner and identifier with your supervising instructor before continuing.

Deliverable: Write a short evidence note covering observed facts, alternative explanations, and the next authorized validation step. No quiz is required.

02 / Endpoint telemetry

Validate visibility on Windows and Linux

The purpose is to see what ordinary administration looks like in local logs. In a real engagement, command execution and collected output can reveal sensitive information. In this course, run only on owned VMs and compare local observations against a synthetic defender record.

Scenario

A defender receives a report that a training process was started. The alert is not proof of malicious behavior. Build a timeline that distinguishes legitimate diagnostic commands, process creation, and an investigator's interpretation.

Practical workflow

  1. On your own Windows VM, collect current OS and selected process information. Do not disable protections or create scheduled tasks.
  2. On a Linux VM, inspect the current identity, selected processes and failed services. A missing systemd command is a platform difference, not an incident.
  3. Record timestamps, machine labels and what log sources are actually available. Never claim a log source was enabled without checking.
  4. Write a defensive analyst note explaining why a process name alone cannot prove malicious intent, and identify two additional fields you would request.

Owned-VM commands

Windows PowerShell (read only):
Get-Date -Format o
Get-Process | Select-Object -First 8 ProcessName,Id,StartTime
Get-Service | Select-Object -First 8 Name,Status

Linux terminal (read only):
date --iso-8601=seconds
id
ps -eo user,pid,comm --sort=pid | head
systemctl --failed --no-pager

Interpretation

Process IDs change across runs; start times can be missing or inaccessible. Service status reflects one instant and does not reveal the actor's intent. Compare with an approved baseline before escalation.

Troubleshooting and limitations: If Get-Process StartTime raises an access error for protected processes, rerun without that field or select an accessible user process. Do not escalate permissions solely to complete the exercise.

Deliverable: Write a short evidence note covering observed facts, alternative explanations, and the next authorized validation step. No quiz is required.

03 / Identity and access

Map least-privilege failures from synthetic audit logs

A red-team report becomes useful when it explains the control weakness and the evidence needed to verify it, not just a dramatic outcome. This exercise uses deliberately fictional access records.

Scenario

At 14:10 UTC a fictional user was added to a privileged group. A change ticket at 14:06 UTC authorized a temporary elevation for a lab migration, but the ticket specifies removal by 14:30 UTC. A second audit entry at 14:47 UTC shows continued group membership. Your task is to assess whether the record indicates a governance gap without testing any real credential.

Practical workflow

  1. List the relevant evidence: approved ticket, membership entry, expiry requirement and later observed membership.
  2. Separate observed violations of policy from speculative explanations, such as an attacker adding the account.
  3. Recommend read-only checks and an owner-approved removal workflow with verification afterward.
  4. Draft a two-sentence finding with impact, evidence, limitations and risk owner. Do not invent a severity score unsupported by context.

Synthetic evidence / worked example

14:06Z CHG-2041 approved elevation of lab_user
14:10Z IAM event: lab_user added to LAB_ADMIN
14:30Z approved expiration time
14:47Z IAM group listing: lab_user remains in LAB_ADMIN
15:02Z analyst flags overdue access review

Interpretation

The observed membership beyond the expiration window supports a potential privileged-access lifecycle failure. It does not establish unauthorized account control, credential theft or actual misuse.

Troubleshooting and limitations: If time zones differ, normalize all records to UTC before comparing the approval window. Ask whether the authorization was extended before declaring noncompliance.

Deliverable: Write a short evidence note covering observed facts, alternative explanations, and the next authorized validation step. No quiz is required.

04 / Network segmentation

Reason about exposure without scanning unrelated hosts

Network routes and firewall rules often provide enough evidence for a preliminary design review. The goal here is to read a fictional policy and identify the smallest safe validation that would confirm whether it works.

Scenario

A test VLAN at 192.0.2.0/24 should reach a synthetic update server on TCP 443 but must not reach the database subnet 198.51.100.0/24. These are documentation address blocks, not live targets. A sample policy accidentally contains a broad allow rule above its database deny rule.

Practical workflow

  1. Read policy rules in order and mark which match the test source, destination and port.
  2. Explain why an earlier broad allow can supersede a later deny in an ordered rule engine, while noting different vendors may evaluate policies differently.
  3. Propose an owner-approved verification in an isolated virtual network; do not probe production ranges.
  4. Document both the intended policy and the observed or hypothetical test result, emphasizing uncertainty if no real test was run.

Synthetic evidence / worked example

Rule 10 ALLOW from TEST_VLAN to ANY tcp/443
Rule 20 DENY  from TEST_VLAN to DB_NET any
Rule 30 ALLOW from TEST_VLAN to UPDATE_NET tcp/443
Default DENY

Interpretation

On a first-match rule engine, traffic to database TCP/443 could match rule 10 before rule 20. If the engine uses another evaluation model, validate its actual behavior rather than applying the conclusion blindly.

Troubleshooting and limitations: If a local VM lab cannot reach the synthetic target, validate VM network adapters, virtual switches and routing before treating the outcome as a successful firewall block.

Deliverable: Write a short evidence note covering observed facts, alternative explanations, and the next authorized validation step. No quiz is required.

05 / Purple-team learning

Turn an exercise into actionable defender improvements

A purple-team session connects the scoped assessment with detection engineering and operations. A good finding names observable signals, false-positive limits and a repeatable validation method.

Scenario

The fictional defenders receive two alerts: a legitimate administrator enumerated services during approved maintenance, and a test account was left in a privileged lab group after expiration. The first should be contextualized; the second should create an accountable review, not automatic accusations.

Practical workflow

  1. Write one detection hypothesis for each event, specifying fields such as actor, timestamp, host, group and approval ID.
  2. State a likely benign cause of the signal and how an analyst can distinguish it from a policy violation.
  3. Describe the least disruptive control change: improved approval linkage, expiry reminders, and review escalation.
  4. Create an observation matrix with evidence available, missing context, proposed owner and a falsifiable acceptance criterion.

Synthetic evidence / worked example

Signal: privileged group membership after approved expiry
Context: ticket ID, approver, account, group, expiration timestamp
Decision: request IAM owner verification; check valid extension
Action: owner-approved revocation if still unauthorized
Validation: expired membership absent; evidence retained; no unapproved access blocked

Interpretation

Success is not merely creating an alert. Success is producing a decision an analyst can explain, with a known false-positive boundary and a safe response path.

Troubleshooting and limitations: Avoid writing rules that block every administrative command. They can disrupt legitimate operations and create noisy detection queues.

Deliverable: Write a short evidence note covering observed facts, alternative explanations, and the next authorized validation step. No quiz is required.

06 / Professional reporting

Write the red-team / defender handoff

Use a reproducible report that another authorized professional could validate. Put confirmed observations ahead of assumptions and separate actions taken from recommendations.

Scenario

Use the previous five fictional exercises to produce an executive summary and technical appendix. Do not claim you compromised a network or demonstrated an exploit: no exploitation or remote target access occurred.

Practical workflow

  1. Executive summary: objective, scope, three principal observations, business impact and remaining uncertainty.
  2. Technical record: environment, exact read-only commands or synthetic data references, UTC timeline and redaction decisions.
  3. Findings: evidence, possible alternate explanations, proposed mitigation owner and a safe verification test.
  4. Debrief: what the defender saw, what the tester expected, why the difference mattered and which controls will be reassessed.

Synthetic evidence / worked example

Finding title: Expired lab privileged membership not automatically reviewed
Observed: approved expiry 14:30Z; membership still present at 14:47Z
Evidence: synthetic CHG-2041 and IAM record
Limitation: no validation of ticket extension performed
Risk: excess privilege persists beyond intended period
Recommendation: owner-led expiry review, automated exception queue
Acceptance: reconcile permissions and tickets in isolated retest

Interpretation

A precise, qualified finding is stronger than an exaggerated one. Reporting should allow the organization to reproduce the evidence without exposing a real user or production service.

Troubleshooting and limitations: If you cannot substantiate an impact statement, label it a plausible scenario instead of a confirmed effect.

Deliverable: Write a short evidence note covering observed facts, alternative explanations, and the next authorized validation step. No quiz is required.
